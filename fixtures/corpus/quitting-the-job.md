# The Morning I Quit, and the Eighteen Months After

I handed in my notice on a Tuesday, which in hindsight was a mistake — Tuesdays have no ceremony to them. You'd think a decision like that deserves a Friday at least. Instead I sat in a glass conference room, said the sentence I'd rehearsed in the car, and my manager just nodded like I'd told him the printer was out of toner.

For nine years I'd done the same general shape of job at three different companies. Different logos, same meetings. I was good at it, which is its own trap. Nobody questions the thing they're good at, even when it's quietly hollowing them out. I used to tell people I was "building toward something," and the honest answer was I had no idea what that something was. I just knew the Sunday dread had started showing up on Saturday.

So I quit, with eleven months of savings and a plan that fit on one index card: open a coffee shop. Not a café, not a "concept" — a coffee shop, the kind with a bell on the door and regulars who don't need to order out loud anymore.

The first six months were humbling in a way I wasn't prepared for, even though everyone warns you. I'd managed budgets in the millions at my old job and I still got the lease negotiation wrong twice. I learned more about health permits than any person should need to know. I learned that "small business owner" mostly means doing the dishes yourself at eleven at night because your one part-time hire called out.

There was a stretch in month four where I did the math on what I'd given up — the salary, the title, the 401k match — and sat in my car in the parking lot and cried for about ten minutes. Not dramatically. Just quietly, the kind of crying where you're also checking your phone. Then I went back inside and finished closing.

Here's what surprised me most: the thing I missed from the old job wasn't the money, or even the stability. It was being handed problems that were already defined. Someone else had decided what mattered. Running your own place means you decide everything, all day, and half your decisions are wrong, and there's no one above you to catch it.

Eighteen months in, the shop breaks even most months and turns a real profit in December. I know every regular's order. I know which ones are going through something just by what they order that week. None of that shows up on a P&L, but it's the whole reason I did this.

Would I tell someone else to do it? Depends on the person. If you're doing it to escape something, it won't work — the problems just change shape. If you're doing it because you've got a specific, almost embarrassingly concrete idea of what you want your days to look like, then maybe. I wanted to know my customers' names. That was basically the whole plan. Turns out that was enough, barely, most weeks.

I still think about that Tuesday sometimes. Not with regret. Just surprised at how ordinary it felt to do something that big.
