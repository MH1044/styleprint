# Learning to Fix Things Without Breaking Them Worse

I used to think "handy" was a personality you were born with. Some people just know which end of the wrench goes where, and the rest of us call a guy. That was me for about thirty-one years. Then the garbage disposal died on a Sunday, every plumber in town was closed, and I had exactly one option: figure it out or live with a sink full of coffee grounds until Monday.

Here's the thing nobody tells you — most household repairs are not complicated. They are just unfamiliar. There's a difference. Complicated means the problem itself is hard. Unfamiliar means you've never opened that particular panel before and your brain treats the whole thing like a bomb disposal. I pulled up three videos, found the reset button on the bottom of the disposal, and it turned back on in about four minutes. Four. I had been avoiding this unit for two years.

That one win did something to me. I started looking at every broken thing in the house like it was a puzzle instead of a verdict. The dripping bathroom faucet. The closet door that wouldn't sit flush. The dishwasher that left everything smelling faintly of wet dog. One by one, I took them apart.

Not everything went well, to be clear. I flooded the kitchen once trying to replace a supply line, because I didn't know you're supposed to shut off the water at the valve first and not just at the fixture. Learned that one the hard way, towels everywhere, wife standing in the doorway with the particular silence that says more than yelling ever could. I still bring it up sometimes just to watch her face.

What changed wasn't skill, really — it was patience with being bad at something in front of myself. Most of us stop doing things the moment we're not immediately competent. We call a professional, order the part online, pay someone forty dollars to turn a screw we could've turned ourselves if we'd given it ten minutes and a YouTube tab. I get why. Nobody wants to spend their Saturday elbow-deep in a cabinet under the sink. But there's a particular kind of confidence that only comes from having broken something, fixed it yourself, and watched it work again.

My rule now is simple: if it doesn't involve gas lines or the main electrical panel, I try it myself first. Worst case, I call the guy anyway and I'm out the cost of a part. Best case, I save eighty bucks and learn something I'll use again in three years when the exact same thing breaks at my sister's place.

The toilet that wouldn't stop running taught me more about flappers and fill valves than I ever expected to know. The ceiling fan that wobbled like it was trying to take off taught me that balancing kits exist and cost six dollars. None of this is hard. It's just slow the first time, and then fast every time after.

I'm not going to pretend I can rewire a breaker box or that I'd trust myself with anything structural. There's a line, and I know where mine is. But the space below that line — the drawers, the drips, the squeaky hinges, the half the house that just needs somebody willing to look closely and ask what's actually wrong instead of guessing — that space got a lot bigger than I expected.

My dad was the opposite of handy too, which I think is half of why I assumed I would be. Turns out it's not hereditary. It's just whether you let yourself be bad at something for one Saturday afternoon.
